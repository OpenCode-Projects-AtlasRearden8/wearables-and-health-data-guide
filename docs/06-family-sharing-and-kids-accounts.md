# Family Sharing and Kids' Accounts

Health data feels warm and familial — "share your activity with Mom!" — until someone realizes the ring of visibility never dims: a parent checking a teenager's sleep score, a spouse achieving morning-workout guilt by proxy, a person in care being nudged hourly by an app that means well. Sharing can join the family up. It can also quietly become surveillance with a friendly font.

This chapter is about drawing the line on purpose: what's worth sharing, what's never worth sharing without a specific yes, and how kids' accounts work when the kid in your care is younger than the app's minimum age.

## The guardrail sentence for the whole chapter

> **Every person in a family — including children and the elderly relatives you help — is an individual whose health data deserves their own consent to be *seen* by anyone else, for as long as they can meaningfully give it.**

Consent gets softer only where the law or the person's own situation requires a guardian to make choices for someone (more in the "someone else's" section below). For everyone else, default permission should be **off, until requested.**

## The usual methods of sharing (and what each one shares)

Family health sharing shows up under a few umbrellas, differing by brand:

| Sharing style | What it usually means | The kind guideline |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Activity-sharing circles** (steps, workouts, friend leaderboards) | Opt-in mutual following of *summaries* — often partial, like steps only | Fun and social; turn it on with a *named* person, and check what visibility labels exist (many apps can hide sleep or exact times) |
| **Family plans / shared subscriptions** | One payment, multiple accounts — often *no data sharing at all* | The safest sharing: all it shares is the bill. Where you have a choice, prefer this over data-sharing by default |
| **Family health summaries** (a dedicated family-health feature) | A dashboard where a designated person can see chosen data of chosen relatives | Design on purpose: which data, anyway? Prefer summary cards (e.g., notifications only on request) to silent monitoring; see below |
| **Shared devices / shared accounts** | Grandpa's watch paired to *dad's* account | Common with older adults, genuinely helpful — but the account owner must be the person whose data it is, wherever that's possible |
| **Emergency/SOS contacts** | Who gets called during a fall/crash-alerts feature | Sensitive and purposeful; set it with the wearer, not for them, and revisit when life changes |

Notice how much of the pie chart of family sharing is *not* about data sharing at all. If the household's goal is "we like the gadget and want one bill," the family-plan style sharing meets it with zero privacy downside.

## The shared-health dashboard, done well

Dedicated family-health features (a parent's alert when a grandparent hasn't worn their watch, movement-noticed notifications, etc.) are the most useful and the most delicate. They work well when designed as **care invitations**, not passive observation:

1. **Define the trigger, not the feed.** "Ping me if Dad's watch hasn't moved by noon" beats "let me watch Dad's movement all day." The first is a fishing net of care; the second is a camera.
2. **Give the wearer the off-switch.** A grandparent can pause sharing without a phone call home. If the feature can't be paused, that's a point against the feature.
3. **Share summaries as default; trends only by request.** Movement liveness *yes*, detailed heart-rate charts by default *no*.
4. **Do the annual review with the [privacy checklist](11-privacy-checklist.md)** — family sharing arrangements drift in the current as members' circumstances change.

## Kids' accounts and youth wearables

Kids' wearables (the "call-me-and-tracks-location" watch band of products) and activity trackers intended for children introduce the biggest consent wrinkle: the child's data is collected *by* the parent, *for* the parent, *about* the child.

The honest landscape, in plain English:

- **Check the age floor.** Most maker-account terms of service have a minimum age (commonly 13, though it varies by country and brand), and some family features require the minor to have their *own* account in the family group with limited controls. Read the brand's family/teen account page before buying the device, not after — it explains what under-threshold kids can and can't have.
- **Prefer kid-specific products where offered.** Several makers have purpose-built children's lines (GPS/call watches with guardian interfaces). These collect *less*: no sleep-stage analysis for a nine-year-old, no cycle tracking, no workout-everything. Under the general rule of "collect the least that serves the purpose," these are the right default for pre-teens.
- **Turn off the feedback loop before it forms.** A child's tracker should *not* be gamified into purposeless goal-pressure (competitive badges for every week). Notification-free by default, activity visible to *them* if they care, no reminders that scold.
- **Body-related data gets a caretaker veto.** Weight, body-fat estimates, and calorie/energy-balance features aimed at anyone — but *especially* a child — deserve to be off entirely. Growth, body image, and food relationships are things to discuss with a pediatrician, not to gamify on a wrist. This is one of the most firm spots in this whole manual.
- **Location has a purpose and an expiry.** Children's location sharing for school-runs safety is a legitimate feature with honest design: **purpose-based, two names' worth of guardians, and scheduled off** (e.g., active on school days, off in evenings), and a conversation about it growing as the kid does. Location sharing *about* a teenager who'd rather you didn't is a family-culture question, not a settings question — but treat the tech's answer (silent tracking) as the wrong default.
- **Teach the data literacy in the moment.** The single best kids' feature you can enable is *conversation*: "the watch asked to track your sleep — want to see what a week looks like?" Health-data literacy at age ten prevents the anxiety loops of chapter [three](03-what-the-numbers-honestly-mean.md) at age fourteen.

## Teen and young-adult accounts

When a teen is old enough for a standard account, the healthy setup is **their own account, their own login, the family plan for billing only:**

- Parent controls live in the *family group* (spending, app approvals, content limits) — where they can be adjusted — rather than through a back door into their health dashboard.
- Sharing circles ("mom can see my runs") can be offered by the teen, revoked by the teen, and — honestly — re-offered on a good week. The relationship survives the toggling; that's what maturity practice looks like.
- Where a teen has *medical* monitoring needs (a clinician-directed heart or sleep issue), the clinician and the family should agree on the Sharing design together. The [care-team chapter's](05-sharing-trends-with-your-care-team.md) one-page summary works beautifully for teens: the teen prepares it, where possible, and their voice owns the visit.

## Helping someone at a distance: eldercare sharing

For an older family member, wearables often serve two goals at once: their independence and your peace of mind. The honest way to hold both:

- **Start from what they want tracked.** Falls, calls-for-help, "didn't get up today" — ask them. Many will say all of the above *plus* "don't need the sleep charts on your phone." That answer is a usable feature spec.
- **The wearing-consistency problem is real.** A health-alert system only works when worn; comfort (strap choice, charger simplicity) is a *medical-safety* decision here, so invest in the comfortable strap. See the [battery chapter](08-battery-habits.md) for the simple charging rhythm that keeps both of you happy.
- **Have a no-surprise rule.** Anything new enabled (a new alert to you, a new zone) gets mentioned at the next visit or call. No silent switches, either direction.
- **Document the arrangement** on paper with both signatures where the stakes are high — who sees what, how to pause it, who else gets called. Families forget specifics; paper doesn't.
- **Keep your own dashboard humble:** your role is *backup*, not 24/7 monitor. If the arrangement starts to feel like management rather than care, hold it against the guardrail sentence at the top of this chapter and adjust together.

## What never to share by default

A short blacklist for the whole household to agree on:

1. **Cycle/reproductive data** — extremely sensitive; share never, or strictly with the person whose data it is (or the clinician they name).
2. **Sleep stage/stress/recovery scores** of other adults — they're [estimates, not truth](03-what-the-numbers-honestly-mean.md), and they're a rich source of household judgment dynamics. Off until asked.
3. **Workout GPS routes** of a minor, a partner, or a person at risk — location history is a safety-sensitive file, not a bragging chart. Keep it off in family dashboards.
4. **Anyone's data into AI tools or third-party apps** on another person's behalf — the [export chapter's](04-exporting-your-data-safely.md) safe-handling rules apply *doubly* to data that isn't yours.
5. **Data after a relationship change** — separation, divorce, a falling-out. Un-share as part of separating digital life; this list is not rhetorical for you if you've seen it happen.

The [privacy checklist](11-privacy-checklist.md) turns this blacklist into pass/fail questions you can actually audit once a season.

## The family conversation, in ninety seconds

Here's a script for a calm household ten-minute talk about wearables, if you want one:

> "The watch company wants us to join things together. Here's what I'd suggest: we can share *steps and streaks* if we each want it — opt-in, one person at a time. Sleep and heart stuff stays private unless we each say otherwise. For the kids, they get [the kid version / their own account] and we see what they're comfortable showing. And for [Grandma], let's set up what she wants noticed, not what we want to watch. Anyone want to change anything?"

It's a strange sign of our times that this conversation now needs to exist — but the surface it sits on (well-meaning defaults plus family-by-default assumptions) is exactly why it does.
