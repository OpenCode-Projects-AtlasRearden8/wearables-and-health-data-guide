# Battery Habits

Battery life is the unsung hero of good health data. A dead watch tracks nothing a dead watch tracks nothing; a watch that's *always* on the charger misses nights; a battery that dies monthly at year three quietly retires a good device early. This chapter is short, practical, and slightly opinionated — because battery care is one of the few places where small habits pay off measurably for years.

## The core rhythm: anchor charging to an existing habit

The kindest battery habit isn't "charge when it dies" — it's a fixed docking time attached to something you already do. Choose an anchor and let the watch note it:

| If your tracker tracks... | Anchor charging in... | Why this slot works |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Sleep | Showers and evening wind-down | 45–90 min docks a typical day's use without touching sleep hours |
| Sleep (rings) | Morning routine (coffee, breakfast) | Rings charge fast; breakfast usually covers a full day |
| Workouts only | Not anchored — charge whenever; workouts don't care | If you don't sleep-track, battery anxiety is optional |
| Night shift / rotating schedule | Anchor to *pre-shift*, and use manual sleep-mode toggles | Scheduled features assume a normal day (see the [setup chapter's shift note](02-setting-up-your-watch-kindly.md)) |

The one non-negotiable in the whole table: **if you sleep-track, the watch needs to be on your wrist during sleep hours** — which is the whole reason anchored charging slots into showers and breakfast, not "before bed whenever."

## The deep habits that make batteries age slowly

Lithium batteries age by three stresses: **deep discharges** (0% dead), **trapped heat**, and (mildly) **sitting at 100% all the time**. You can't dodge all three with an always-worn device, but you can soften each:

1. **Top-ups beat drain-cycles.** A twenty-minute top-up here and there is easy on the battery. Repeatedly running it to empty is not. If the device will die anyway tonight, that's fine — just don't make 0% a lifestyle.
2. **Heat is the real enemy.** Never leave the device charging in a hot car, on a windowsill in the sun, or under a pillow. Charge it where air moves. A watch charged at 35°C (95°F) ages faster than the same watch charged at room temperature — no battery chemistry is subtle about this.
3. **Use the official or a well-reviewed charger.** Knock-off charging docks are the most common cause of swollen batteries and intermittent charging. The genuine article costs little and lasts years.
4. **Keep the contacts and strap clean.** Wipe sweat/salt off the back sensor and charger contacts every week or two. Corrosion slowly degrades charging sessions long before the battery itself is weak — this single habit rescues more "my battery died!" devices than any other.
5. **Battery-saver modes are for dead-end situations** (multi-day hikes, travel with no charger) — not a default, because they usually trade away the [continuous readings that make trends work](03-what-the-numbers-honestly-mean.md).

> **Longevity notes for the second and third year:** expect real capacity to fade gradually — a device that lasted 7 days new may last 4–5 by year three. That's normal aging, not a defect. When charging stops holding a *day*, or the battery swells (the case lifts from the glass, or the strap pressure feels odd), it's time to check the maker's battery-replacement service before buying a new device — it's often cheaper and always kinder to the planet.

## The daily-wearer's cheat sheet

Small moves that add hours:

- **Always-on display → raise-to-wake.** The single biggest battery consumer at the display level. (Keep always-on *off* except when you specifically want a watch face.)
- **Screen brightness at the lowest you can live with** — auto-brightness handles most situations.
- **Turn off sensors you don't use** (background SpO2, skin-temperature sampling where optional): the [privacy chapter's](07-privacy-settings-worth-touching.md) E-section doubles as a battery chapter here, because sensors not running don't drain anything.
- **GPS workouts are the hunger moments.** Long recorded hikes with route tracking eat battery fastest by design; charge anchor accordingly on big-outing days.
- **Airplane mode / theater mode for stretches you won't want alerts** (movies, deep-work blocks, mowing — theater mode is a mowing champ) — the watch stops pinging the phone constantly, sometimes for a day's worth of savings.

## Charging with kindness for others

For battery duty across a household — a kid, an older relative, a person with ADHD:

- **Two chargers, one price.** A second dock costs almost nothing and turns "did you charge it?" into a non-conversation. Nightstand plus kitchen or office = the habit forms itself.
- **Anchor charging to *their* routine, not yours** — a 9-year-old's watch charges during screen time, a grandparent's during their 10 o'clock nap-with-coffee. Same principle, no micromanaging.
- **Visual state beats verbal reminders.** Most apps show charge state on the watch face; a dead watch is louder than a nagging parent. Pair the dock with something they never forget: "watch sleeps where the toothbrush lives."
- **When the battery dies overnight (sleep tracker edition):** don't panic and don't skip logging anything in the logbook — the wearable's sleep data is reconstructed only from worn hours; a missed night just shows as a gap in the trend, which is exactly what happened in reality. Note it in your [monthly ritual](09-the-monthly-data-ritual.md) if the gadget's trend has *gaps you'd rather remember the cause of*, then move on.

## When batteries die entirely

The capsule summary of end-of-life wearables:

- **Check the maker's battery-replacement service first** — several official programs exist; a fair fraction of "dead" watches are just worn batteries.
- **Where no service exists**, honest options: certified electronics recyclers (not the bin), manufacturer trade-in programs (several makers give credit toward a NEW device), or resale to a repair hobbyist.
- **Know what leaving a device means for your data**: unpairing + factory-reset the device before parting with it, and delete the account/data afterwards if that's what your [privacy checklist](11-privacy-checklist.md) says you promised yourself. Export your history first, as covered in the [export chapter](04-exporting-your-data-safely.md).

## The one-sentence battery policy

> **Dock it at the same life-anchor daily; keep it cool and clean; use features you actually use — and the battery will quietly handle the next few years.**
