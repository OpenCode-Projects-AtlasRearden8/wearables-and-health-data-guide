# What the Numbers Honestly Mean

This is the chapter people wish gadget reviews contained. Every wearable marketing page shows beautiful graphs; every wearable measures something *estimated* through skin, with motion in the way, by a sensor the size of a lentil. That's fine — you just need to know where each number sits between "clinical-grade" and " vibes."

The one-sentence summary of this entire chapter, worth taping somewhere:

> **A single reading is a sketch. A trend over weeks is a story. Only your care team can tell you what the story means for you.**

Here's the honest ladder, from most to least trustworthy, followed by a careful tour of the most common readings.

| Trust tier | Reading | Honest description |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Solid | Steps | Counts arm motion; good for comparing *yourself* to *yourself*, wrong at the margins |
| Solid | Sleep timing (asleep vs. awake) | Very good at "you were asleep then," wrong at stage level (see below) |
| Fair | Total sleep time | Reasonably close to reality when worn consistently |
| Fair | Resting heart rate | A real, meaningful number when measured at the same conditions daily |
| Fair | Workout heart rate, average HR | Good enough for pacing yourself; not lab-grade |
| Estimate | Sleep *stages* (deep/REM/light) | Educated inference from movement and pulse — never clinical sleep staging |
| Estimate | Heart-rate "zones," calories burned | Modeled guesses keyed to your age/weight settings |
| Estimate | HRV, stress/readiness/recovery scores | Derived composites; useful as *your* baselines, meaningless across people |
| Estimate | SpO2 (blood oxygen) spot checks | Not a pulse oximeter your care team would defend in court |
| Vibe | "Temperature variation" on watches, "illness detection" | Relative-to-your-own-baseline trends only; no absolute temperature you can hand to a clinician |

An estimate is not a lie. It's a number whose error bars the maker believes are acceptable for the purpose — but the purpose is *looking at yourself over time*, not diagnosing anything.

## Heart rate: what a wrist can actually tell

The optical sensor (PPG) flashes green light at your skin and watches how much blood absorbs it with each heartbeat. Strong points:

- **Resting heart rate** — your heart rate when calm and still, most usefully as seen during sleep — is one of the best numbers a wearable produces. When worn nightly and measured the same way, your own trend is genuinely informative: many wearables will show you a 90-day baseline, and the interesting question is always "how is today *different from mine*?" (A rising pattern can be explained by many ordinary things — late meals, caffeine, alcohol, a head cold, hard training, travel. That's exactly why the trend belongs in a conversation with your clinician, not in a panic at 2 a.m.)
- **Zone workouts and average heart rate** are good enough to pace by. The classic wrist problem — brief, sharp spikes or drops — happens most during activities with lots of arm bending (weightlifting grips, downhill running) and cold weather, when blood flow to the skin drops.
- **Chest straps and cuff measurements remain more accurate** for workouts where you're actually training on the number, and for anything you'd care to write in your logbook.

What the wrist cannot do: tell you what a heart rate reading *means clinically*. A resting number of X might be perfectly ordinary for you and remarkable for someone else. Wearables can flag a pattern — "unusually high resting heart rate for several days" — and that flag is a *prompt to mention to your own clinician*, not an alarm to act on alone.

> **Honesty note about 2 a.m. numbers:** most wearables will happily show you your heart rate graph for any moment of the night. If you ever find yourself checking heart rate at 2 a.m. and worrying, that's not a data problem, it's an anxiety loop, and the kind fix is the notification rules in the [setup chapter](02-setting-up-your-watch-kindly.md) plus taking questions to people, not to graphs.

## Sleep: interesting, not diagnostic

Sleep tracking on wearables works by combining **movement analysis (actigraphy)** with **pulse timing** to guess whether you were asleep and, roughly, which flavor of asleep. The honest report card:

- **Getting "was I asleep and when did I wake" right:** yes, quite good. If the graph shows you woke at 4:50 and fidgeted for half an hour, it likely did.
- **Total sleep duration:** decent, with one systematic bias: wearables tend to award themselves a few minutes of slop they call "light sleep," especially the first moments of stillness in bed before you're truly asleep, and especially if you read in bed.
- **Stages (light/deep/REM):** the science is real — clinicians do stage sleep with brain-wave equipment in a lab — but no wrist device has that equipment. Your wearable's stage split is best understood as a *shape, not a chart*: did this week's sleep look more similar to last week's? Did REM-shaded nights cluster after late screens and late alcohol? Relative pattern — useful. Absolute stage minutes — decoration with error bars.
- **"Sleep score" numbers:** a proprietary spaghetti of duration, timing, consistency, movement, and pulse, weighted by company preference, scaled to company taste. Two good nights on the same real sleep can give different scores on different brands. Use them as a *consistency nudger*, never as a grade.

The most defensible sleep metric in the whole industry costs nothing: **what time did I fall asleep, and what time did I wake?** If your wearable helps you keep those two facts tidier, it has already earned its battery charge.

## The derived scores: recovery, readiness, stress, HRV

You'll meet a family of scores with calm names and no clinical meaning: *readiness*, *body battery*, *recovery*, *stress*. Almost all are built around **heart-rate variability (HRV)** — the tiny millisecond-to-millisecond flutter of your pulse — plus sleep and motion data.

- HRV itself is real physiology, and for a consistent wearer measuring the same way, *your own baseline* is the useful reference. A week of readings far *below* your usual baseline can simply mean "your body is working through something" — late nights, alcohol, illness, hard training.
- The scores that package HRV into one number are **snapshots of your labor, not verdicts on your body.** Two wearables reading the same wrist will disagree. Never compare yours to someone else's, ever.
- If a readiness score tells you to skip a workout on Wednesday and your body is saying go — the body wins. These tools serve consistency, not authority.

For the same reason, **HRV belongs in the reflection pile of your [monthly data ritual](09-the-monthly-data-ritual.md), not the daily-checking pile.** The daily-checking pile is where anxiety lives.

## Blood oxygen (SpO2) and the "temperature" stories

- **SpO2 spot checks** on watches estimate the oxygen saturation in your blood using red and infrared light. They are not clinical pulse oximeters. They can miss many things that matter, in cold hands and poorly-fitting positions especially. A watch SpO2 reading is a curiosity, never a clinical number, and you should never act on one alone — this is one of the few places in this manual that says that sentence twice.
- **Night-time "temperature variation" or "temperature trends"** on smartwatches and rings measure the *relative* skin temperature against your own recent baseline — good for spotting that "something changed this week" (travel, illness, a fever pattern, cycle tracking where supported). Not useful as a fever thermometer: a true body temperature still needs a real thermometer, logged in the [vitals logbook companion](05-sharing-trends-with-your-care-team.md) if you track it.

## Steps, activity, calories

- **Steps** count wrist motion, so a shopping trip while pushing a stroller can undercount, and a door-knocking errand can overcount. Fine. Use it to compare months, not minutes.
- **Calories burned** is the least trustworthy number in the whole ecosystem: it's modeled from your self-reported body stats plus motion plus heart rate, with error bars so wide they belong in a physics joke. Use "active minutes" instead of calories wherever your app offers the choice.
- **Standing/workout detection** is decent for runs, walks, and bikes, weaker for swimming (short strokes confuse it) and strength work (grip movements look like rest). Log workouts manually when it matters — manual entries are a *better* signal than auto-detect, and they're cheaper to fix later.

## The habit that makes the whole chapter work

None of these numbers improves with more checking. Every one of them improves with **consistency**: same wearing position, same sleep window, same measurement time, then *weeks* of data.

That's why this book keeps steering you toward:

1. A [gentle monthly review](09-the-monthly-data-ritual.md) instead of daily graph-poking;
2. A [one-page data-decode worksheet](10-data-decode-worksheet.md) where you capture what's actually actionable;
3. [Sharing with your own care team](05-sharing-trends-with-your-care-team.md) when a *question* forms — not when a number arrives.

When a wearable sticks to those lanes — inform, notice, and hand you a good question — it's a genuinely useful little machine. When it starts handing out verdicts, that's the moment to reread this chapter from the top.
